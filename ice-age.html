Ancient timeline

The age of ice

This page expands the age of ice card. The ice line in the margin marks the science side. The milestones are the last long cold, told from places a modern reader can find on a map, and from what a person would have met there.

About 120,000 to 11,500 years ago

One glacial

NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information place the most recent glacial period between about 120,000 and 11,500 years ago. After it, the Earth is in the interglacial called the Holocene. Glacial periods are colder, dustier, and generally drier. When more of the world’s water is locked in ice, more land stands clear of the sea.

NOAA, Glacial-Interglacial Cycles

About 125,000 years ago

Britain an island, and warm

The Natural History Museum’s account of how Britain was made: about 125,000 years ago Britain is an island. The sea stands higher than today and covers low land, including parts of Norfolk and Lincolnshire around the Wash. Hippos wallow in the River Thames. Lions live where Trafalgar Square stands. Britain is left without people.

Natural History Museum, The making of an island

About 60,000 years ago

A grass plain in place of the sea

Sea level drops as water freezes into ice caps and glaciers. A vast grass-covered plain joins England to the continent. Neanderthals return across Doggerland, the Museum’s name for a landscape now under the North Sea. The plains carry herds of woolly mammoth, woolly rhino, and reindeer, and also wolves and hyenas. The Museum’s account of those Neanderthals on the Doggerland steppe gives winter temperatures averaging about −10°C. Trees are scarce, so wood for fuel and for the shafts of stone tools is scarce. A jaw from a cave near Torquay, on the south coast, shows Homo sapiens in Britain within 20,000 years of the Neanderthals’ return.

Natural History Museum, The making of an island Natural History Museum, First Britons

About 26,500 to 19,000 years ago

The thick ice, late in the same cold

The University of California, reporting the Bering Strait study, dates the Last Glacial Maximum from about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago. Ice sheets cover large areas of North America. Average global sea level is about 130 meters lower than today.

AntarcticGlaciers.org, on the Laurentide Ice Sheet, places that coldest interval between about 26,000 and 20,000 years ago. The sheet covers most of present-day Canada and reaches south into the northern United States, as far as the Great Lakes region, parts of the Midwest, and the north-eastern United States. The ice is thickest over central Canada, on the order of 3 to 4 kilometres over the Hudson Bay region. The Cordilleran Ice Sheet bounds it on the west. The climate is drier than today. On unglaciated ground beside the ice, the country is cold, open steppe-tundra, with woolly mammoths.

The same lower sea uncovers the continental shelves and leaves Beringia dry: a vast tract from Siberia to Alaska, with herds of horses, mammoths, and other Pleistocene animals. The land bridge has been in place since about 35,700 years ago, and it is still there through the height of the ice. The University of California notes that some studies suggest people may have lived in Beringia through that height.

In Britain the Natural History Museum calls about 20,000 years ago the most severe part of this glacial. Ice covers much of Britain. From about 25,000 years ago humans disappear, and that absence lasts more than 10,000 years.

About 13,000 to 11,000 years ago, and the millennia after

The straits fill again

As the ice sheets melt, the Bering Strait floods again around 13,000 to 11,000 years ago. The walk from Siberia to Alaska is sea once more. Over the past 12,000 years the risen sea draws the outline of Britain that a modern map shows. Doggerland is the ground that ends under the North Sea. NOAA’s glacial sheet places the Earth, after this cold, in the Holocene.
