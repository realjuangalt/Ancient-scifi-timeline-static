The Æsir–Vanir War

This page expands the timeline card. The gold line in the margin marks the myth side. The ancient gods-war is the first war between the Æsir and the Vanir. Ragnarök is the end-time war, labeled separately below.

Overview

The first war in the world

In the Poetic Edda’s Völuspá, the seeress remembers the first war: Gullveig is burned in Odin’s hall and lives again; Odin hurls a spear; the Vanir break the gods’ wall. Britannica’s Vanir article says the Vanir demanded reparation or equal status after Gullveig’s torture; war followed; equality was granted; hostages were exchanged—Njörðr and Freyr to the Æsir, Hœnir and Mímir to the Vanir. Snorri’s Ynglinga saga tells a euhemerized version with the same exchange.

Völuspá, Bellows 1923 Britannica: Vanir Ynglinga saga

In the story Beat 1

Gullveig burned three times

The war I remember, the first in the world, When the gods with spears had smitten Gollveig, And in the hall of Hor had burned her,— Three times burned, and three times born, Oft and again, yet ever she lives.

Völuspá 21, Henry Adams Bellows, 1923. Wikisource.

In the story Beat 2

Odin’s spear and the broken wall

On the host his spear did Othin hurl, Then in the world did war first come; The wall that girdled the gods was broken, And the field by the warlike Wanes was trodden.

Völuspá 23, Bellows 1923. Same page. (Bellows’s stanza numbering places the council stanza beside the battle.)

In the story Beat 3

Peace and hostages

Britannica: after defeats, the Æsir grant equality. The Vanir send Njörðr and Freyr; they receive Hœnir and Mímir. The peace ritual that mingles saliva produces Kvasir. In Ynglinga saga chapter IV, Odin’s army and the Vanaland people ravage each other until they exchange those hostages.

Britannica: Vanir Ynglinga saga IV

End-time war Separate note

Ragnarök

Later in the same Völuspá, the seeress turns to the fate of the gods: fire and flood, Odin’s fall, the wolf and the serpent, then a green earth rising again. Bellows’s introduction calls this final battle ragna rök, the fate of the gods—the end-time war, distinct from the first war of Æsir and Vanir.

Völuspá, Bellows

Composition or tablet date

When the text is dated

Bellows’s 1923 introduction places the Völuspá poet chiefly in Iceland in the middle of the tenth century, with writing centuries later. The Codex Regius preserves the poem; another version stands in the Hauksbók (about 1300). Snorri’s Heimskringla is thirteenth century.

Bellows introduction
