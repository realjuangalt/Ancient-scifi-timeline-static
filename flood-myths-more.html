Other flood stories

This page is a separate expansion. The stories here are off the spine.

The one memory told as Noah, Atrahasis, Ziusudra, and Utnapishtim stays on the timeline. Noah dates that flood only to his 600th year. Plato’s Atlantis stays on the timeline too. Only that flood self-dates near 9600 BCE. The fuller tellings, including the spine, are on the flood stories page.

NorseUncalibrated

Ymir’s blood

Source: the Prose Edda, Gylfaginning. This flood is at the making of the world.

Before the earth is shaped, the giant Ymir is killed by the sons of Bor. His blood runs out in such a flood that it drowns the frost giants. Bergelmir gets away with his household. He is a giant, not a man, and the giants who come after are said to come from him. Only then is the earth built, from Ymir’s body: flesh as soil, bones as mountains, blood as the sea.

Norse: Ymir’s blood is a creation flood. The story places the blood before the world has a shape.

IranianUncalibrated

Yima’s enclosure

Source: the Vendidad, the story of Yima.

Ahura Mazda warns Yima that a killing winter is coming, a cold that will destroy cattle and people. Yima is told to build a vara, a sealed enclosure, and to bring into it the best of men and women, the best of animals, and the seeds of plants, two of each kind that should continue. He seals it. Inside, life goes on through the winter. There is no ship, no sea, and no mountain landing. The world outside is buried in cold.

Iranian: a sealed enclosure against winter. The story sets this in Yima’s reign, in the early age of the world.

GreekDeduced placement

Deucalion and Pyrrha

Source: the Greek flood of Deucalion, told in Apollodorus and in Ovid.

Zeus resolves to destroy the people of the Bronze Age. Prometheus warns his son Deucalion. Deucalion and his wife Pyrrha survive in a chest, or in a small boat, and the water carries them to Mount Parnassus. When the flood sinks, they ask how people can be made again. They are told to throw the bones of their mother behind them. They understand the mother to be the earth, and the bones to be stones. The stones Deucalion throws become men. The stones Pyrrha throws become women.

A Greek flood, mapped here to the same Mesopotamian memory. The story calls it the flood of that age.

Maya · K’iche’Uncalibrated

The wooden people

Source: the Popol Vuh. Do not fold this into the Aztec story of a couple in a log. They are different tellings.

The gods have tried to make people. The wooden people can speak and multiply, but they have no hearts and do not remember their makers. A flood is sent against them. Resin rains down. Animals they had used turn on them. Their grinding stones and pots rise up. The wooden people are washed away and destroyed. Two of them, Tata and Nene, are told to hide in a hollow cypress and to eat nothing but what they are given. They come out too soon and cook food. For that they are changed into dogs.

Maya and Aztec: repeating world-age endings. The story sets this in the attempts before the people of maize. It does not give a year.

AztecUncalibrated

The ages that end

Source: the Aztec telling of the world-ages, the suns, one after another.

The world does not flood once. An age ends, and another age begins, and that happens again. One of those ages ends in water. The people of that sun are swept away, and the next sun is made after them. The destruction is a rhythm. Jaguar, wind, fire, and flood take their turns, and the present sun is the one that followed.

Repeating world-age endings. The story counts the ages.

HopiUncalibrated

The climb from below

Source: Hopi emergence tellings.

The people are living in a world under this one, and that world floods. They do not sail. They climb. Tall plants, reeds, grow up through the water toward an opening in the sky of that world, which is the ground of this one. The people ascend the plants and come out into the new world. Those who climb are the ones who continue.

Climbing out of a flooded underworld on plants. The story tells it as a change of worlds. It does not give a year.

ChoctawAs written down

The boat

Source: a Choctaw flood story as it was written down.

A flood covers the land. People survive by a boat. The story, as collected, is a boat story: a warning, a vessel, and people carried through the water until there is ground again.

Shaped by later collectors. Choctaw, the later Hawaiian houseboat, Maori, and Yoruba are flagged by collectors as missionary-shaped or Muslim-shaped. The people who wrote this version down noted that it may reflect missionary influence. That is their note. The story itself gives no year.

Hawaiian · an older reportUncalibrated

Two people on Mauna Kea

Source: an older Hawaiian report, distinct from the houseboat.

A flood leaves two people alive on Mauna Kea. They are asked about a ship. They say they never heard of one. There is no boat in their account. They simply remained on the mountain when the water was gone.

Two people on Mauna Kea, no ship. The report gives no year for the flood.

Hawaiian · a later tellingAs written down

The houseboat

Source: a later Hawaiian telling, in which Nuʻu rides out a flood.

A flood comes over the islands. Nuʻu builds a houseboat, a vessel with a house on it, and he survives in that. When the water falls, he is the one left to begin again, and he makes an offering.

The later Hawaiian houseboat is one of the stories flagged by collectors as missionary-shaped. The people who wrote this version down noted that it may reflect missionary influence. That is their note. The houseboat and the Mauna Kea report are two tellings.

IrishNoah’s dating, borrowed

Cessair

Source: the Lebor Gabála Érenn, the Book of Invasions. The story means this to be Noah’s flood.

Cessair, of Noah’s line, is told that Ireland is a place the flood has not yet reached. She sails there with her company to get ahead of it. They land forty days before the water arrives. When the flood comes, it is Noah’s flood, reaching Ireland late. The company dies. The story remembers one man, Fintan, left alive after them.

Irish: Noah’s flood on purpose, said to arrive forty days early. The dating borrows Noah’s.

HinduUncalibrated

Manu

Source: the earliest telling is in the Shatapatha Brahmana. Later puranas stretch the same rescue across a cosmic age.

A fish comes to Manu and asks to be saved. Manu keeps it, and it grows from a jar to a tank to the river to the ocean. The fish tells him a flood is coming, and that he must build a ship. When the water rises, the fish, now huge, tows the ship by a rope to a northern mountain. Manu is the one left to begin the world again. In the later telling, it is the dissolution at the end of an age, and that age is spoken of in billions of years.

A cosmic-age story. The earliest version is a fish and a ship. The billion-year pralaya is later. This page does not date it. The early story does not give a year. The vast span belongs to the later cosmic telling.

ChineseTraditional late 3rd millennium BCE

Yu

Source: the traditional story of Yu the Great, set at the beginning of the Xia.

The rivers have overflowed and the land is under water. Yu’s father, Gun, tries to dam the water and fails. Yu does not build a boat and does not ride the flood out. He drains it. He cuts channels, dredges the rivers, and walks the land until the water has a way to the sea. The work takes years. He passes his own door and does not go in. When the land is dry enough to live on, he is remembered as the founder of the Xia.

River drainage, traditional late 3rd millennium BCE. A deduced placement. Tradition places that work at the start of the Xia, in the late third millennium BCE. The story itself tells it as the labor that made the kingdom possible.
