Ancient timeline

Göbekli Tepe

This page expands the timeline card. The ice line in the margin marks the science side. The card uses the range UNESCO prints for the monuments: 9,600–8,200 BCE.

Where and when

The Germuş mountains

UNESCO places Göbekli Tepe in the Germuş mountains of south-eastern Anatolia, in Upper Mesopotamia. The German Archaeological Institute's project page puts it in the Şanlıurfa region of southeastern Turkey, on one of the highest plateaus of the Germuş Mountains, around 770 meters above sea level, about 15 km northwest of the city of Şanlıurfa. The Institute's Tepe Telegrams FAQ puts the same tell about 15 kilometres to the northeast of Şanlıurfa, on the highest point of that range. The FAQ describes a man-made mound about 9 hectares in area, some 300 metres across, and up to about 15 metres high.

UNESCO's short description dates the monumental round-oval and rectangular megalithic structures to between 9,600 and 8,200 BCE, in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. The statement of outstanding universal value dates the same building to the 10th–9th millennia BC. The World Heritage Committee inscribed the property in 2018, on criteria (i), (ii), and (iv).

The Institute's project page dates the site to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B, from about 9,500/9,250 to 8000/7,750 calBC. The Tepe Telegrams FAQ, from radiocarbon ages on organic remains in the fill of the enclosures and on wall plaster, assigns the oldest layers to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, written there as approx. 9.600–8800 calBC, and to the early and middle Pre-Pottery Neolithic B, approx. 8.800–8.000 calBC. The FAQ says the filling of the mound ended around 8.000 calBC.

What they built

Pillars and enclosures

UNESCO describes monumental communal buildings, or enclosures, of limestone, with distinctive T-shaped pillars, some up to 5.50 meters tall. Some pillars are abstract depictions of the human form, with low reliefs of belts and loincloths, and with high and low reliefs of wild animals. The pillars were carved from the adjacent limestone plateau. The Institute's project page describes the same T-shaped pillars, up to 5.5 m high, some richly decorated, inside monumental round-oval buildings.

The Tepe Telegrams FAQ describes the special buildings as monolithic T-shaped pillars set in a circle around a central pair of larger pillars, more than 5 metres tall and also T-shaped. Most carry animals, mainly in low relief and sometimes in high relief: smaller wild species, many birds, predators and especially fox, spiders, and insects. Several pillars have arms and hands. In two, the hands rest on a belt from which a loincloth hangs. The project reads the T-shaped pillars as anthropomorphic statues.

The FAQ says four comparatively large circular special buildings, A to D, have been partially excavated. In C and D the floors were made by smoothing the natural rock, and the central pillars stand in sleeves cut from that rock. Building B has a lime-plaster floor, called a terrazzo floor. Building E, on the western plateau, keeps the carved foundations and the sleeves for its central pillars; its pillars were not preserved. Buildings F, G, and H were found later and were under excavation when the FAQ was written.

The people

Hunter-gatherers, and a settlement

UNESCO says groups of hunter-gatherers erected the monuments. The short description says the monuments were probably used in connection with rituals, most likely of a funerary nature. The statement of outstanding universal value says they were probably used in connection with social events and rituals, and that recent excavation has also found non-monumental structures which appear to stem from domestic buildings. The imagery, UNESCO says, gives insight into the way of life and beliefs of people in Upper Mesopotamia about 11,500 years ago.

For the first twenty years of the dig, the Institute's project page says, Göbekli Tepe was read as a primarily ritual site, a mountain shrine, because of those round-oval buildings and the T-pillars. Newer excavation, the same page says, has produced evidence of domestic activities and of a permanent settlement. The project now reads the site as a settlement with a pronounced ritual component.

The FAQ says the enclosures were built and used by hunter-gatherers who, following the movements of wild animals, were half-nomadic, and who were beginning to take up settled and later food-producing ways. Later finds named there include fire installations, remnants of consumed animals and grains, structures read as cisterns, burials, and many buildings. The FAQ also notes depictions of vultures, hyenas, human heads, and headless bodies, motifs known at neighbouring sites in connection with burial rites, and an avifauna heavy in scavenging corvids, with vultures frequent. The animal reliefs, the FAQ says, depict species that lived near the site. The same motifs appear at other Neolithic sites in the region, on stone vessels, arrow-straighteners, and other objects.

Still in the ground

What the surveys leave buried

The FAQ says only a small part of the site has been excavated. Geophysical work, including ground-penetrating radar, suggests that numerous special buildings with T-shaped pillars remain hidden. The project excavates only as much as it needs in order to answer questions still open, because digging also takes archaeological contexts apart.

A 2018 Tepe Telegrams account of the surveys of 2003, 2006, 2007, and 2012 quotes Klaus Schmidt's 2003 field report: more than ten large enclosures could be located on the geomagnetic map, and some more could be expected. With four enclosures then under excavation, A to D, a minimum of 20 enclosures seemed to exist inside the mound, and more than 200 pillars could be calculated if each enclosure held about twelve. Later surveys, the post says, found the monumental circular enclosures all over the tell, not in one part of it. In 2018 Jens Notroff wrote that large-scale excavation was not then planned for the areas still under trees, and that the age of structures not yet excavated cannot be known until they are dug. The shapes on the survey maps, he wrote, resemble the older circular monuments, as a guess from analogy.

Excavation began in 1995, after Klaus Schmidt, with M. Akman and M. Morsch, recognized the site in a 1994 survey. The FAQ traces the work from Schmidt and the German Archaeological Institute, with the Urfa museum, through Schmidt's death in 2014, a team led by Müslüm Ercan of the Şanlıurfa Museum and Lee Clare, and, in 2020, directorship with Necmi Karul of Istanbul University, Clare coordinating. From 2021 the FAQ counts the site in the Tas Tepeler project, with Karahantepe and other early Neolithic sites in the Şanlıurfa region. UNESCO notes that since 2014 the excavation permit has been with the Şanlıurfa Museum in collaboration with the German Archaeological Institute.
