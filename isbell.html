Snake predation and vision

The Fruit, the Tree, and the Serpent

Lynne Isbell, The Fruit, the Tree, and the Serpent, 2009, Harvard University Press. Peterson dates it 2011 in 12 Rules for Life and spells her Lynn.

Thesis: snake predation over tens of millions of years selected for sharper vision, larger visual brains, and fast threat detection. She names the koniocellular path, the superior colliculus, the pulvinar, the parvocellular path, and the amygdala as part of an older fear system.

Fruit: her 2006 paper says ripe fruit or nectar was a requirement and a cause of the visual brain. Sugars, in her account, offset the metabolic cost. Her later essay says fruit allowed the vision, and that it began before upright walking and tool-making.

Later and separate: main brain-size increases after about 2 million years ago. Aiello and Wheeler, 1995, point to animal foods. Early bipedalism about 7 to 4 million years ago, still argued.

Support: a 2013 Davis news report. Macaque neurons responded faster and more strongly to snake pictures than to faces or shapes, in monkeys raised without seeing a real snake. This line is the university news story.

Criticism: Wheeler, Bradley, and Kamilar, 2011, Journal of Human Evolution. No link between forward-facing eyes and a shared history with venomous snakes. Species less likely to be snake prey had more orbital convergence. Snake-spotting may be a side effect of stereoscopic vision.

Labeled as his

Peterson's own addition

Peterson's own addition, labeled as his: the snake gave us the vision of God. In 12 Rules for Life he names her, spells her Lynn, and dates the book 2011. The ripe-fruit sentence beside that is footnoted to other researchers.

Peterson discusses her book with interviewer Jordan Levine (Transliminal), in the talk other pages call Religion, Myth, Science, Truth. The YouTube title calls it a 2014 interview.
