The Tower of Babel

The Babel card opens this page. The gold cards follow the story, from Genesis through the later tellings. The ice cards follow the ziggurat of Babylon and the numbers people have given the tower's height.

Genesis 11:1–9

The story

“And the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech.” Journeying from the east, the people find “a plain in the land of Shinar; and they dwelt there.” They make brick for stone and slime for mortar.

“Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.”

The LORD comes down to see the city and the tower and says, “Behold, the people is one, and they have all one language; and this they begin to do: and now nothing will be restrained from them, which they have imagined to do. Go to, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another’s speech.”

“So the LORD scattered them abroad from thence upon the face of all the earth: and they left off to build the city. Therefore is the name of it called Babel.”

Genesis 11, King James Version

Context

After Noah, and Nimrod

Genesis 10 is the Table of Nations: the families of Shem, Ham, and Japheth spreading out, closing with “by these were the nations divided in the earth after the flood.” Babel follows at once in Genesis 11:1–9, and then the generations of Shem that lead to Abraham. The builders are the flood survivors’ children.

Shinar is the plain of southern Mesopotamia. Genesis 10:10 names Babel there as the beginning of Nimrod’s kingdom: “And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.” The Hebrew Babel is Babylon. Genesis 11 itself names no leader for the tower.

Josephus, in the first century CE, gives it one. In Antiquities 1.4 Nimrod, “the grandson of Ham, the son of Noah,” turns the people against God, and says “he would build a tower too high for the waters to be able to reach.” In his telling the tower grew very high, and its great thickness made it look lower than it was. He gives no measurement.

Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews, Whiston translation Genesis 10–11, KJV

A Sumerian parallel

Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta

A Sumerian epic tells of Enmerkar, lord of Uruk, who wants the people of Aratta to bring stone and build a great shrine for Inana. His messenger chants the “incantation of Nudimmud,” in which the lands, “the many-tongued,” address Enlil “in a single language,” and Enki “shall change the speech in their mouths.”

Scholars read that passage in two ways: as a memory of one language being split, or as a wish for all lands to speak as one. The translation is fragmentary in places. The epic does not describe a tower.

Archaeology

Etemenanki, the ziggurat of Babylon

Etemenanki, “Temple of the Foundation of Heaven and Earth,” was the ziggurat of Marduk in Babylon. Some scholars propose it as the building behind the Babel story. Its first construction is uncertain; Andrew George places it between 1500 and 1000 BCE. Sennacherib destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE, and Nebuchadnezzar II rebuilt the tower in the sixth century BCE.

Herodotus describes it in Histories 1.181: “a solid tower measuring a furlong both in length and in breadth, and on this tower another tower has been erected, and another again upon this, and so on up to the number of eight towers.” He does not say he saw it himself.

The Esagila tablet, a copy made at Uruk in 229 BCE of an older text, gives a height of about 91 meters on a base about 91 meters square. Some engineers argue mudbrick could not stand that tall and propose about 66 meters with the top temple. Alexander ordered it cleared for a rebuild that never came, and today little more than the foundation outline remains.

The numbers

“Three times taller than anything today”

Genesis gives no height. Two later texts do. The Book of Jubilees 10:21, in R. H. Charles’s 1913 translation: “its height amounted to 5433 cubits and 2 palms.” At a cubit of 0.45 to 0.52 meters that is about 2.4 to 2.8 kilometers. Taking an 18-inch cubit gives 2,484 meters, three times the 828 meters of the Burj Khalifa.

The Greek Apocalypse of Baruch, 3 Baruch 3, says God confused their speech “when they had built the tower to the height of four hundred and sixty-three cubits,” about 212 meters. Britannica adds Gregory of Tours, in the sixth century, at 200 cubits. The Talmud gives no height; in Sanhedrin 109a Rabbi Yohanan says a third of the tower was burned, a third swallowed into the earth, and a third still stands.

The line “three times taller than the Burj Khalifa” comes from Wikipedia. Its Tower of Babel article quoted the Jubilees figure for years. An anonymous edit on 29 November 2010, the year the Burj opened, added the conversion and the comparison, and that wording is still in the article. Blogs, videos, and glossaries repeat it from there.

A modern reading

All that was left

On X in October 2026, Open Minded Approach asked why everyone at Babel spoke one language, and answered: “they were all that was left.” In his reading they were “the breakaway civilization that survived the Geophysical Event,” the kind of planet-wide catastrophe the ECDO theory describes. “One group of survivors, one language.” He guesses the next survivors will speak English or Mandarin.

Genesis also places Babel among the flood survivors’ descendants. The catastrophe in his reading is physical, where Genesis has God’s flood.

Jubilees 10:21

Where the 5,433 cubits come from

R. H. Charles’s translation: “And they built it: forty and three years were they building it; its breadth was 203 bricks, and the height (of a brick) was the third of one; its height amounted to 5433 cubits and 2 palms, and (the extent of one wall was) thirteen stades (and of the other thirty stades).” The brackets are his. Verse 26 adds that “the Lord sent a mighty wind against the tower and overthrew it,” and that they called it “Overthrow.”

The text gives the number and nothing behind it: no source and no working. In his 1902 notes, Charles says he made three slight emendations to this verse. He fills the bracketed parts from a Greek quotation in the Catena of Nicephorus, which also gives the height in cubits and palms. A newer translation of the Ethiopic gives the brick width as 13 units and runs the thirteen stades into the height. The exact numbers depend on which manuscripts you follow.

Charles collects later echoes. Jerome cites the “stadium” of the tower from the Little Genesis. Eutychius of Alexandria gives walls 5,533 fathoms high and a tower 10,000 fathoms high. A Muslim legend in Weil’s collection has Nimrod’s tower reach 5,000 cubits. Charles offers no explanation for 5,433, and I found no scholar who explains it.

A modern reading

Too big to fail

The psychologist Jordan Peterson reads Babel as a warning about systems that grow until they try to replace heaven. In his 2017 lecture on the story he says: “It should have been so big it had to fail. And that’s what I think the story of the Tower of Babel is about. It’s a warning against the expansion of the system until it encompasses everything. It’s a warning against totalitarian presumption.”

On the scattering, he says that people “create towers of babble and the story indicates well those things collapse under their own weight and everyone goes their own direction.” He calls it “a calling for a kind of humility of social engineering.”

Peterson, Biblical Series VIII: The Phenomenology of the Divine, transcript

What we don’t know

The gaps

Genesis is the earliest telling. Jubilees, Josephus, 3 Baruch, and the Talmud retell it and add details. Enmerkar is a separate Sumerian story about tongues. No independent Mesopotamian record of the Babel tower event has been found, and no ruin is confirmed as the tower of the story. Etemenanki is the leading candidate, but the building Herodotus and the Esagila tablet describe is the sixth-century BCE rebuild under Nebuchadnezzar II. Jubilees and 3 Baruch do not say where their heights come from.
